## Title
Architecture as a Cognitive and Selective Niche: Toward an Evolutionary Architectonics

## Abstract (verbatim)
Human beings do not merely inhabit their environments; they actively construct, modify, and transmit them across generations. Through niche-construction theory, evolutionary biology has established that organisms can alter the environmental conditions under which they and subsequent generations develop and evolve. Yet the specific contribution of architecture to this process, particularly through its organisation of human perception, cognition, and behaviour, warrants further interdisciplinary investigation.

This paper proposes evolutionary architectonics as a conceptual framework for examining the reciprocal relationships between architectural environments and human capacities. Bringing together evolutionary biology, anthropology, archaeology, cognitive science, neuroscience, and architectural theory, it investigates how built environments function not only as material and cultural products of human activity, but also as environments that structure the conditions of human development and adaptation.

We explore the proposition that architectural configurations organise recurrent perceptual, cognitive, and behavioural demands, influencing processes such as spatial perception, attention, memory, navigation, bodily coordination, and social interaction. Through repeated engagement, these environments may also participate in developmental processes, shaping the acquisition, exercise, and maintenance of capacities. From a niche-construction perspective, this raises the further question of how architectural environments interact with cultural transmission, ecological inheritance, and, potentially, longer-term evolutionary processes.

Building upon existing research on niche construction, embodied cognition, environmental scaffolding, developmental plasticity, and architectural perception, the paper examines the possible mechanisms connecting spatial organisation, environmental interaction, and human adaptation. It further considers how archaeological and anthropological evidence might illuminate the historical relationships between built environments, cultural practices, and human populations, while distinguishing developmental and cultural processes from biological evolutionary change.

This contribution seeks to establish an interdisciplinary conceptual and methodological framework for investigating these relationships across immediate, developmental, and generational timescales. Ultimately, it asks whether architecture should be understood not only as an expression of human capacities and cultural adaptation, but also as an active component of the environments through which human capacities are developed, maintained, and potentially transformed over time.
